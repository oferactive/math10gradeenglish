1. Statements and theorems

A theorem is a statement that has been proved. Most theorems have the form "if (hypothesis) then (conclusion)". For example:

"If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then its diagonals bisect each other."

Hypothesis: the quadrilateral is a parallelogram. Conclusion: its diagonals bisect each other.

A proof is a chain of steps from the hypothesis to the conclusion, where each step is justified by a definition, an axiom or a theorem proved before. A few examples, however many, are never a proof.

Example 1: a proof written in steps

Prove that the diagonals of a parallelogram \(ABCD\) bisect each other.

Solution.

StatementReason
\(AB\parallel DC\), \(AB=DC\)properties of a parallelogram
\(\angle BAO=\angle DCO\), \(\angle ABO=\angle CDO\)alternate angles between parallel lines
\(\triangle ABO\cong\triangle CDO\)ASA (angle, side, angle)
\(AO=CO\), \(BO=DO\)matching sides of congruent triangles

Congruence theorems used in proofs: SAS (two sides and the angle between them), ASA (two angles and the side between them), SSS (three sides), and a fourth one you will meet in Unit 18.

2. The quantifiers "for every" and "there exists"

"For every triangle, the sum of the angles is 180°" is a claim about all triangles. To prove it we need a general proof. To disprove it, one counterexample is enough.

"There exists a rectangle whose diagonals are perpendicular" is a claim about at least one object. To prove it, one example is enough (a square). To disprove it, we must show that no object works.

StatementIts negation
For every \(x\), \(P(x)\) is true.There exists an \(x\) for which \(P(x)\) is false.
There exists an \(x\) for which \(P(x)\) is true.For every \(x\), \(P(x)\) is false.
Example 2

True or false: "For every number \(x\), \(x^2\ge x\)."

Solution.

False. Counterexample: \(x=\tfrac12\): \(\tfrac14\lt \tfrac12\).

The order matters. "For every number \(x\) there exists \(y\) with \(y\gt x\)" is true (take \(y=x+1\)). "There exists \(y\) such that for every \(x\), \(y\gt x\)" is false: there is no largest number.

3. A theorem and its converse

The converse of "if \(P\) then \(Q\)" is "if \(Q\) then \(P\)": hypothesis and conclusion change places. A true theorem can have a false converse.

TheoremConverseIs the converse true?
If a triangle is isosceles, its base angles are equal.If two angles of a triangle are equal, it is isosceles.Yes
If \(C=90^\circ\), then \(c^2=a^2+b^2\) (Pythagoras).If \(c^2=a^2+b^2\), then \(C=90^\circ\).Yes (proved in Unit 18)
If two angles are vertical angles, they are equal.If two angles are equal, they are vertical angles.No: base angles of an isosceles triangle
If \(x=2\), then \(x^2=4\).If \(x^2=4\), then \(x=2\).No: \(x=-2\)

When both a theorem and its converse are true, we combine them with "if and only if": "A triangle is isosceles if and only if two of its angles are equal."

The contrapositive "if not \(Q\) then not \(P\)" always says the same as the original theorem. "If it rains, the ground is wet" means the same as "if the ground is dry, it is not raining".

4. Necessary and sufficient conditions

If "\(P\) implies \(Q\)" is true, we say:

  • \(P\) is a sufficient condition for \(Q\): knowing \(P\) is enough to be sure of \(Q\).
  • \(Q\) is a necessary condition for \(P\): without \(Q\), \(P\) cannot happen.

If \(P\) implies \(Q\) and \(Q\) implies \(P\), then \(P\) is a necessary and sufficient condition for \(Q\) ("if and only if").

Example 3

Square and rectangle.

Solution.

"Being a square" is sufficient for "being a rectangle" (every square is a rectangle), but not necessary (a \(2\times5\) rectangle is not a square).

"Being a rectangle" is necessary for "being a square", but not sufficient.

Example 4

A necessary and sufficient condition.

Solution.

A quadrilateral is a parallelogram if and only if its diagonals bisect each other. (Example 1 proves one direction; workbook question 13 proves the other.)

5. Proof by contradiction

To prove a statement by contradiction:

  1. Assume that the conclusion is false.
  2. Reason correctly from this assumption.
  3. Reach something impossible (a contradiction with the hypothesis or with a known fact).
  4. Conclude that the assumption was wrong, so the conclusion is true.
Example 5

Prove that a triangle cannot have two right angles.

Solution.

Assume a triangle has two right angles. Then these two angles already add up to \(180^\circ\), and the third angle is \(0^\circ\), which is impossible. So a triangle has at most one right angle.

Example 6

Prove that \(\sqrt2\) is not a fraction.

Solution.

Assume \(\sqrt2=\tfrac pq\), a fraction in lowest terms. Then \(p^2=2q^2\), so \(p^2\) is even, so \(p\) is even: \(p=2m\).

Then \(4m^2=2q^2\Rightarrow q^2=2m^2\), so \(q\) is even too. But then \(\tfrac pq\) was not in lowest terms: a contradiction.

6. Common mistakes

  • Proving a "for every" statement by checking examples. Examples can only show that something is false, never that it is always true.
  • Using the converse of a theorem as if it were the theorem itself. Check whether the converse has been proved.
  • Negating "every triangle is acute" as "no triangle is acute". The correct negation is "there is a triangle that is not acute".
  • Mixing up necessary and sufficient. "Sufficient": enough to guarantee. "Necessary": cannot happen without it.
  • In a proof by contradiction, assuming the wrong thing: you must assume the opposite of the conclusion.
  • Writing a step without a reason. In a geometry proof every statement needs a justification.

7. Check yourself

Short exercises to check that you understood the explanation. Try each one before you open the solution. For more practice, go to the workbook.

  1. What is the hypothesis of "If \(x\gt 2\) then \(x^2\gt 4\)"?

    Show solution

    \(x\gt 2\)

  2. Write the converse of "If \(x\gt 2\) then \(x^2\gt 4\)". Is it true?

    Show solution

    "If \(x^2\gt 4\) then \(x\gt 2\)". False: \(x=-3\).

  3. Give a counterexample to "Every prime number is odd".

    Show solution

    \(2\)

  4. Negate: "Every triangle is acute".

    Show solution

    There is a triangle that is not acute.

  5. Is "\(x=3\)" sufficient for "\(x^2=9\)"? Is it necessary?

    Show solution

    Sufficient, yes. Necessary, no: \(x=-3\) also gives \(x^2=9\).

  6. What is the first step of a proof by contradiction?

    Show solution

    Assume that the conclusion is false.

  7. What does the congruence theorem SSS say?

    Show solution

    Two triangles with three pairs of equal sides are congruent.

  8. Complete: "A triangle is isosceles if and only if…"

    Show solution

    …two of its angles are equal.
