1. What is a locus?

A locus (plural: loci) is the set of all points that have a certain property. To prove that a figure is the locus, we must show two things:

  1. every point of the figure has the property;
  2. every point with the property lies on the figure.

Missing either direction leaves the proof incomplete.

Property of the pointLocus
at distance \(r\) from a point \(O\)the circle with centre \(O\) and radius \(r\)
equally far from two points \(A\) and \(B\)the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\)
equally far from the two sides of an angle (inside the angle)the bisector of the angle
at distance \(d\) from a line \(\ell\)two lines parallel to \(\ell\), one on each side
equally far from two parallel linesthe parallel line midway between them
Example 1: the perpendicular bisector

Prove that the locus of points equally far from \(A\) and \(B\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\).

Solution.

Let \(M\) be the midpoint of \(AB\).

Direction 1. \(P\) on the perpendicular bisector: \(AM=BM\), \(\angle PMA=\angle PMB=90^\circ\), \(PM\) common. \(\triangle PMA\cong\triangle PMB\) (SAS), so \(PA=PB\).

Direction 2. \(PA=PB\): \(AM=BM\), \(PM\) common, so \(\triangle PMA\cong\triangle PMB\) (SSS). Then \(\angle PMA=\angle PMB\), and together they make \(180^\circ\), so each is \(90^\circ\): \(P\) is on the perpendicular bisector.

2. The circumcentre: perpendicular bisectors

Theorem. The three perpendicular bisectors of the sides of a triangle meet at one point \(O\). It is the centre of the circumscribed circle, the circle through the three vertices.

Proof. Let \(O\) be the meeting point of the perpendicular bisectors of \(AB\) and \(BC\). By the locus, \(OA=OB\) and \(OB=OC\). So \(OA=OC\), and \(O\) is on the perpendicular bisector of \(AC\) too. The circle with centre \(O\) and radius \(OA\) passes through all three vertices.

Where is \(O\)? Inside an acute triangle, at the midpoint of the hypotenuse of a right triangle (so \(R\) is half the hypotenuse), and outside an obtuse triangle.

3. The incentre: angle bisectors

Theorem. The three angle bisectors of a triangle meet at one point \(I\), which is equally far from the three sides. It is the centre of the inscribed circle, the circle that touches all three sides. \(I\) is always inside the triangle.

Proof. The bisectors of \(A\) and \(B\) meet at \(I\). \(I\) is equally far from \(AB\) and \(AC\) (on the bisector of \(A\)), and equally far from \(AB\) and \(BC\) (on the bisector of \(B\)). So it is equally far from \(AC\) and \(BC\), and lies on the bisector of \(C\).

Area and the inscribed circle. Joining \(I\) to the vertices splits the triangle into three triangles with height \(r\): \(S=\tfrac12ar+\tfrac12br+\tfrac12cr=r\cdot s\), where \(s\) is half the perimeter.

4. The centroid: medians

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Theorem. The three medians meet at one point \(G\), the centroid (centre of mass). \(G\) divides each median in the ratio \(2:1\), counted from the vertex.

Idea of the proof (uses the midsegment, Unit 20). \(D\) and \(E\) are the midpoints of \(BC\) and \(AC\), so \(DE\parallel AB\) and \(DE=\tfrac12AB\). Triangles \(GDE\) and \(GAB\) are similar with ratio \(1:2\), so \(GD=\tfrac12GA\). The same holds for every pair of medians, so all three pass through the same point.

In coordinates, the centroid is the average of the vertices: \(G\left(\dfrac{x_A+x_B+x_C}3,\ \dfrac{y_A+y_B+y_C}3\right)\).

5. The orthocentre: altitudes

Theorem. The three altitudes of a triangle (or the lines containing them) meet at one point \(H\), the orthocentre.

In an acute triangle \(H\) is inside; in a right triangle it is the vertex of the right angle; in an obtuse triangle it is outside.

Example 2

Find the orthocentre of \(A(0,0)\), \(B(6,0)\), \(C(2,4)\).

Solution.

Altitude from \(C\) (perpendicular to the x-axis): \(x=2\).

\(m_{BC}=\dfrac{4}{-4}=-1\), so the altitude from \(A\) has slope 1: \(y=x\).

\(H(2,2)\). Check with the altitude from \(B\): \(m_{AC}=2\), slope \(-\tfrac12\): \(y=-\tfrac12(x-6)\), and at \(x=2\), \(y=2\) ✓

6. The fourth congruence theorem and the converse of Pythagoras

Fourth congruence theorem (SSA). If two sides and the angle opposite the larger of them are equal in two triangles, the triangles are congruent.

If the angle is opposite the smaller side, the triangles may not be congruent: there can be two different triangles with the same data. This is the "two triangles" case of the law of sines (Unit 16).

Converse of Pythagoras' theorem. If \(c^2=a^2+b^2\), the angle opposite \(c\) is right.

Proof. Build a right triangle with legs \(a\) and \(b\). Its hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=c\). The two triangles have the same three sides, so they are congruent (SSS), and the angle opposite \(c\) is right.

Example 3

Is the triangle with sides 9, 12, 15 right-angled?

Solution.

\(9^2+12^2=81+144=225=15^2\). Yes: the right angle is opposite the side 15.

7. Common mistakes

  • Proving only one direction of a locus.
  • Mixing up the centres: circumcentre (perpendicular bisectors, circle through the vertices) and incentre (angle bisectors, circle touching the sides).
  • Thinking the perpendicular bisector of a side passes through the opposite vertex. That is true only in special triangles (for example the base of an isosceles triangle).
  • Writing the 2:1 ratio of the centroid the wrong way: the longer part is between the vertex and \(G\).
  • Using SSA as a congruence theorem when the angle is opposite the smaller side.
  • Checking Pythagoras with the wrong side: \(c\) must be the longest side.

8. Check yourself

Short exercises to check that you understood the explanation. Try each one before you open the solution. For more practice, go to the workbook.

  1. What is the locus of points at distance 5 cm from a point \(O\)?

    Show solution

    The circle with centre \(O\) and radius 5 cm.

  2. Which lines meet at the centre of the circumscribed circle?

    Show solution

    The perpendicular bisectors of the sides.

  3. Which lines meet at the centre of the inscribed circle?

    Show solution

    The angle bisectors.

  4. In which ratio does the centroid divide a median?

    Show solution

    \(2:1\), the longer part from the vertex.

  5. What is the meeting point of the altitudes called?

    Show solution

    The orthocentre.

  6. A right triangle has hypotenuse 10. Find \(R\).

    Show solution

    \(R=5\): the circumcentre is the midpoint of the hypotenuse.

  7. Is a triangle with sides 5, 12, 13 right-angled?

    Show solution

    \(25+144=169\). Yes.

  8. Which special points are always inside the triangle?

    Show solution

    The incentre and the centroid.
